01 / Problem → solution

Getting started
shouldn’t be this hard.

Bring campus tools, course rules, and human support into one place.

The problemThe proposed solution

Too many places to start.

Your files are in one place, the software in another, and getting access takes another step.

→

One place to begin.

Open your course or project with its files and software ready to use.

Rules are easy to miss.

AI help may be fine for practice and off-limits for the assignment due tomorrow.

→

Know what help is allowed.

Show the rules alongside each task, and test that connected tools follow the limits we can enforce.

Access is uneven.

Some people can afford better devices and paid subscriptions. Others can’t.

→

Enough to do the work.

Fund the tools people need for the task. A personal subscription or a newer laptop should be optional.

An answer isn’t understanding.

A correct-looking answer doesn’t tell you whether someone understands it.

→

Work people can explain.

Make time to check the answer and explain the reasoning. Bring in a peer, instructor, or colleague when needed.

More detail +
01 / Get unstuck

Find the next step.

Problem You have tried the assignment but do not know why your approach fails.

Solution Use the help your instructor allows for this task. Try a hint or a question that helps you spot the problem, then have another go.

How it would work Show your attempt → identify the sticking point → test one change. Ask a peer or TA, sharing only the parts you want them to see.

You leave with A corrected attempt and an explanation of why it works.

What matters here Instructor rules determine the help available. Private tutoring conversations do not become grading evidence.

Did it help? Solve a similar problem independently.

02 / Prepare to learn

Find out what you’re missing.

Problem Rereading notes feels familiar, but you are unsure what you can actually explain.

Solution Use approved course materials to practice questions, compare explanations, and check your understanding.

How it would work Choose a topic → answer before seeing help → check against the source → revisit missed ideas.

You leave with A focused study plan, source references, and questions for office hours.

What matters here Practice is separate from an active assessment. Generated explanations can be wrong and must remain checkable.

Did it help? Explain the concept in your own words without the tool.

03 / Build a project

Start with a ready workspace.

Problem You spend the evening fixing software instead of working on the assignment.

Solution Open a course workspace with the files and software installed, and see how much computing time you have.

How it would work Open the starter → run an example → develop your approach → save a checkpoint. Use permitted debugging help when needed.

You leave with A working notebook or project that can be reopened and checked.

What matters here The course must provide enough computing to complete required work. Paid accounts stay optional, and warnings should arrive before a limit interrupts your work.

Did it help? Reopen the saved work and reproduce its output.

04 / Finish responsibly

Submit work you can defend.

Problem A polished answer may still contain unsupported claims, missing steps, or mistakes.

Solution Review the work against the assignment, check sources and results, and describe assistance where required.

How it would work Check evidence → revise → explain key choices → submit your work and check that it arrived.

You leave with A submission, a clear record of what was submitted, and an explanation of your reasoning.

What matters here The student owns the final work. A checklist or AI review does not certify correctness or academic integrity.

Did it help? Answer follow-up questions and demonstrate the relevant skill.

Teaching

Your course.
Your call.

Decide where AI belongs in your class.

For examplePrepare an activity with clear rules for AI help and an alternative where needed.

You leave withA lesson ready to teach and assess.

More detail +
01 / Design an activity

Begin with the learning goal.

Problem Turning an idea into materials, examples, and a workable activity takes substantial preparation.

Solution Adapt a reusable workspace and, if useful, draft examples, practice tasks, and rubrics from instructor-approved sources.

How it would work Set the learning goal → draft the activity → check accuracy and accessibility → trial it as a student.

You leave with Materials you’ve checked, the resources students need, and an agreed alternative without AI where needed.

What matters here The instructor approves the materials and checks source permissions before release.

Did it help? A learner can start the activity and demonstrate the intended skill.

02 / Set the rules

Put the rules beside the work.

Problem One course policy cannot explain every distinction between practice, collaboration, and assessed work.

Solution Set help levels for each activity: no generative AI, hints and checking, or disclosed collaboration.

How it would work Select task rules → preview the student experience → test allowed and disallowed requests → publish the expectations.

You leave with Rules for the activity that students can read and see reflected in the tools.

What matters here Connected controls must be tested. The system cannot prevent all use of outside tools.

Did it help? Students can explain what help is allowed, and connected tools respect the configured limits.

03 / Support the class

See where students need help.

Problem Repeated setup and concept questions compete with time for individual teaching.

Solution Offer approved practice support and a clear route from self-help to peers, TAs, and the instructor.

How it would work Publish checked guidance → receive the questions students choose to share → clarify recurring issues for the class.

You leave with Questions to address in office hours, materials to improve, and help requests you can follow up on.

What matters here No routine access to private tutor chats or student profiling from help-seeking behavior. Any summary reporting needs explicit privacy rules.

Did it help? Requests reach the right person and students can continue their work.

04 / Assess understanding

Review evidence of learning.

Problem A finished assignment may not show how well a student understands the work.

Solution Combine submitted work with explanations, checkpoints, and short tasks that ask students to apply what they learned on their own.

How it would work Define the evidence → review the submitted work → ask follow-up questions → give feedback and record the instructor’s decision.

You leave with An assessment you can explain using the criteria students were given.

What matters here Grades and misconduct judgments remain human decisions. Private tutoring data and AI-detector scores are not proof of misconduct.

Did it help? The evidence supports the learning outcome and students can understand or question the decision.

03 / Keep the evidence

Make the next decision easier.

Export the runnable example, comparison results, costs, timings, and failures. A promising first result still needs checking.

Set limits before you start.

Use approved data and destinations, isolated workspaces, and a clear stop limit. A prototype does not authorize changes to live university systems.

Retain run settings, failures, and resource use. Note when a provider cannot pin a model version or report exact cost. A successful small test still needs broader validation.

Everyday work

A first draft
you can work with.

Save preparation time. You make the final call.

For exampleTurn approved instructions into a welcome email. Check the details before sending.

You leave withA useful message and a reusable template.

More detail +
01 / Find an answer

Start with the right source.

Problem Routine questions require searching several pages, guides, or procedure documents.

Solution Find relevant passages in approved materials and draft an answer with links and source dates.

How it would work Ask the question → open the cited passages → resolve conflicts with the document owner → respond.

You leave with A checked answer and a reusable reference.

What matters here If sources are missing or disagree, ask the person responsible for the policy. A generated answer cannot determine eligibility or override policy.

Did it help? Another staff member can verify the answer against the authoritative source.

02 / Prepare communications

Draft once. Review carefully.

Problem Rewriting instructions and responding to recurring questions takes time away from direct support.

Solution Turn approved facts into a clear email, FAQ, checklist, or announcement for the intended audience.

How it would work Select approved content → draft → check names, dates, tone, and accessibility → obtain the required approval → send.

You leave with A reviewed message and a reusable template.

What matters here No automatic sending or invented institutional commitments. Include personal information only through an approved data route.

Did it help? Recipients can identify the action, deadline, and person to contact.

03 / Check routine data

Catch errors before they travel.

Problem Duplicates, inconsistent categories, and missing fields slow routine reporting.

Solution Run documented checks on approved data and prepare a list of exceptions for human review.

How it would work Work on a permitted copy → run checks → compare counts and totals → review proposed corrections.

You leave with A list of issues to review and checks you can run again.

What matters here Do not silently overwrite source records or let a model decide financial, personnel, or student outcomes.

Did it help? Every accepted correction is traceable to evidence and reconciles with the source.

04 / Improve a process

Try a small improvement first.

Problem A promising automation idea is hard to evaluate before committing to a larger project.

Solution Prototype one repetitive step using public, synthetic, or otherwise approved examples.

How it would work Describe today’s process → build a small example → compare time and errors → review the result with the process owner.

You leave with A working example, a record of where it falls short, and a decision about whether to keep going.

What matters here A prototype has no authority to write to live systems. Deployment needs an owner, access controls, and a recovery plan.

Did it help? The improvement saves effort without increasing errors or shifting work onto someone else.

A say in the decisions

You belong
at the table.

Help shape campus decisions without using AI.

For examplePropose human-led discussion and feedback, with the time and support it needs.

You leave withA written decision and someone to follow through.

More detail +
01 / Do the work

A complete workspace, with AI off.

Problem A person wants the computing and course materials without sending their work to a generative model.

Solution Let people turn AI off and still use the files, notebooks, software, and submission tools.

How it would work Choose AI-off for the task → work with standard tools → save and submit normally. Instructors can also configure AI-off activities.

You leave with Your completed work and confirmation that it was submitted.

What matters here This mode must make no generative-model calls or hidden uploads. Ordinary hosting still processes files under disclosed terms; it is not offline by default.

Did it help? Complete the task with model access disabled, without prompts to upgrade or enable AI.

02 / Meet the same goal

Agree on another way to learn.

Problem An activity asks students to use AI, but a student objects to using it or cannot access it.

Solution Agree a route that demonstrates the learning outcome through conventional tools, provided examples, or human collaboration.

How it would work Discuss the outcome early → agree scope, support, and assessment criteria → complete the alternative.

You leave with An agreed alternative and a clear understanding of how it will be assessed.

What matters here The instructor must resolve essential AI-specific learning requirements. This proposal does not claim a blanket institutional exemption.

Did it help? The alternative has comparable rigor and a reasonable workload, rather than becoming a penalty for opting out.

03 / Reach a person

Reach a person directly.

Problem Support becomes inaccessible when a chatbot is the only doorway.

Solution Offer direct peer, TA, instructor, or staff contact alongside checked guides and ordinary search.

How it would work Choose human help → select what context to share → see who will answer and when to expect a reply.

You leave with Someone responsible for your question and a way to follow up.

What matters here An AI answer must not be passed off as human help. Someone needs to staff this service, with hours people can rely on.

Did it help? Get assistance without first conversing with an AI system.

04 / Shape the decisions

Help us stay human.

Problem An opt-out alone gives people no say in decisions that affect teaching, work, and campus life.

Solution Give people who choose not to use AI a standing place in university decisions, including proposing ways to protect human teaching, relationships, and judgment.

How it would work Propose a change, such as protecting peer discussion, funding human support, or keeping an activity AI-free → review it with the campus team → receive a written decision and reasons.

You leave with A proposal considered in a university decision, a written response, and a named person responsible for following through.

What matters here Participation does not require using or endorsing AI. Choosing not to use AI must not count against someone in a performance review.

Did it help? Participants can point to decisions their proposals changed, including support for human alternatives or limits on AI.

Proposed pilot experiences. Every option must be accessible, including working without AI.

What we would hold ourselves to +

Say where the work goes. Show what help is allowed and which service receives the data. Never quietly switch to a less appropriate service.

Leave decisions with people. Instructors set course rules. People review the work. There must be a workable option without AI.

Share the decisions. Give people who choose not to use AI a standing role in reviewing results and proposing how to preserve human teaching, relationships, and judgment.

Be willing to stop. Limit or remove features when the costs or risks outweigh the benefit. Private chats stay out of routine review.

Give it a specific job

Decide what to share.
Check what comes back.

  1. Choose the task. Show what help is permitted and whether a personal route is allowed.
  2. Approve the handoff. Preview the selected files, destination, available tools, usage limit and stop time.
  3. Run within those limits. A Wildcat software toolkit (SDK) would connect supported tools, pass along only the permissions needed for the task, and let you cancel.
  4. Bring back the result. Check the returned work and a short record of the service used, task, time, and available usage data. Unrelated chat history stays private.
What the pilot would need to prove +

Your AI account doesn’t grant campus access. A personal sign-in never grants course membership, access to protected files, or authority to submit. Institutional permissions control those actions.

Each person keeps their own account. Use supported per-user authentication, protected storage, short-lived task permissions and disconnect controls. No shared pool of personal accounts.

Limits must actually work. Test data access, cancellation, usage limits and revoked access for each route. If an adapter cannot enforce required controls, keep it out of governed coursework.

Some work will happen elsewhere. When a direct connection isn’t available, let people export permitted material with their approval and bring selected work back. Assess the work and the person’s understanding. We cannot watch every outside interaction or reliably detect AI use.

These connections are proposals, not features you can use yet. Provider documentation checked 10 November 2026. No paid personal account is required for the proposed baseline; no automatic switch to personal billing when campus capacity runs out.

04 / The pilot

Start small.
See what we learn.

A small classroom pilot first.
A supported initial Wildcat AI service targeted for November 2027.

Can students explain their work, researchers repeat a test, and staff save time? That’s what the pilot needs to show.

First 4 weeks*

Connect & test

Test one complete task with fictional learner accounts. Then watch people try it and see where they get stuck.

About 6–10 weeks*

Try it in a class

Check that sign-in and help work, costs stay manageable, and people can recover when something fails.

By November 2027*

Decide what to expand

Use the pilot results to choose the next courses and teams, with a funded university team responsible for the service.

*These targets assume a staffed team, the necessary access, and approval to proceed. Parts of the system have been tested; the complete campus service has not.

A university team, with shared decisions.

A campus team would own the service, budget, and rules. Students, faculty, researchers, and staff would help shape them.
